# Step 9: Governance and decision-making

## Why it matters for us
Three households, six people, friendships, and real money. Most co-op failures are people failures, not finance failures. Decide *how* we decide before we need to.

## Two layers
- **Formal (legal):** board of directors, officers, annual meeting, member votes on big items (amending bylaws, selling, refinancing, admitting members). Required by the entity type. The lawyer drafts it.
- **Everyday (house culture):** chores, guests, pets, shared spaces, small spending.

Radish found it useful to separate **owner decisions** (capital, refinancing, sale) from **resident decisions** (daily life). Even when everyone is both, knowing which hat you're wearing helps.

## Voting structures to consider
How we vote is still an open question. This section lays out the main options so we can compare them. These are choices we will make together, not recommendations. Two questions shape everything else:

1. **Who gets a vote?** Each household, each person, or each share of money put in.
2. **How many votes does a decision need?** Most votes, a larger share, everyone, or simply no strong objection.

Our legal form limits what we can choose. A housing cooperative corporation usually gives one vote per membership. An LLC (the Radish model) can set almost any rule in its operating agreement. Ask the lawyer which of these our entity can support.

### Who gets a vote

| Option | How it works with us (3 households, 6 people) | Strengths | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One vote per household** | 3 votes. Each couple agrees privately, then casts one vote. A majority is 2 of 3. | The classic co-op rule ("one member, one vote"). Simple. Treats each home equally, whatever its size or how much money it put in. | One couple can always be outvoted by the other two. Each couple needs its own way to settle disagreements, and a split inside a couple is invisible to the group. |
| **One vote per person** | 6 votes. A majority is 4 of 6. | Every adult has a direct voice, and disagreements inside a couple are visible. A couple can't win alone: a majority needs at least one person from another household. | A 3–3 tie is possible, so we need a tie-break rule. Couples may simply vote as blocs, which makes it the same as household voting with more steps. The bylaws may need each person to be a member. |
| **Weighted by money put in** (equity-weighted) | Votes in proportion to each household's capital. Before the later household joins, the two founding households hold 100%. | Matches risk to control. Familiar to banks, and common in LLCs like Radish. | Cuts against "community over profit." A household that put in more can outvote others on things like rules about guests and pets. Not usually possible in a true co-op corporation. |
| **Split by topic** | Money and property decisions are weighted by equity. Daily-life decisions get one vote per person or household. | Radish's "owner hat vs. resident hat" idea. Each kind of decision is governed by the people most affected. | More complex, and someone has to decide which category a question falls into. |

### How many votes it takes

| Method | How it works | Strengths | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple majority** | More than half: 2 of 3 households, or 4 of 6 people. | Decisions get made, and a stalemate is impossible with 3 votes. | In a group this small, a majority can feel like two households ganging up on one. |
| **Supermajority** | For example, 5 of 6 people. (With 3 households, anything above a majority means all three.) | Protects against narrow wins on big decisions. | With household voting it becomes the same as unanimity. |
| **Unanimous / consensus** | Everyone agrees. | Strongest buy-in. Good for decisions you can't undo, like selling the building or changing the resale formula. | One person can block everything. Needs a fallback rule if consensus fails, such as mediation and then a majority vote. |
| **Consent** (as in sociocracy, a consent-based governance method) | A proposal passes unless someone has a reasoned objection ("Can you live with it?"). Objections are worked into a better proposal. | Faster than consensus but still inclusive. Popular in co-ops and intentional communities. | Needs practice and a facilitator. An "objection" needs a clear definition so it isn't just a veto. |

### Ways to combine them
- **Tiers.** Different decisions use different rules. See the decision ladder below. Most co-ops do this.
- **A deadlock plan.** Agree in advance what happens if a vote is stuck: a cooling-off period, then outside mediation, then (only for the biggest questions) a fallback such as a majority vote or a buyout process.
- **The later household before it joins.** Options include an advisory voice with no vote, a non-voting seat at meetings, or a vote limited to decisions it will inherit (for example the resale formula or the bylaws). Some groups also give it a veto over anything that would change its future buy-in price.
- **Everyone is the board.** In a co-op this small, every member often serves on the board of directors, so "board decisions" and "member decisions" are made by the same people.

## A decision ladder (adapted from Supernuclear)
The methods below are only examples, to show how tiers work. Fill in our own once we've chosen a voting structure.

| Kind of decision | Example | One possible method |
|---|---|---|
| Small and reversible | Buy a new rake | Whoever's doing it decides |
| Routine spending under a limit | Repairs under $X | Designated person or committee |
| Budget, carrying charges | Annual budget | Majority |
| Membership | Admitting a new household | Consensus or consent |
| Fundamental | Sell, refinance, change the bylaws or equity formula | Unanimous |

## Things to plan for
- **Conflict resolution:** an agreed process before anything goes to a vote or a lawyer, such as talking directly, then a facilitated conversation, then outside mediation.
- **Member who stops paying:** reminders, a payment plan, then formal steps under the occupancy agreement. In Illinois, a co-op can use eviction law against a member ([SELC summary](https://co-oplaw.org/illinois-cooperative-law/)), but have the friendly process first.
- **Roles:** treasurer and bookkeeper, maintenance coordinator, secretary (keeps the [decision log](../worksheets/decision-log.md)), exemption filer. Rotate them over time.
