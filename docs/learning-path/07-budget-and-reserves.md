# Step 7: Monthly budget, carrying charges, and reserves

## Why it matters for us
The monthly budget is where a co-op lives or dies. Underfunded reserves are the most common way co-ops get into trouble: the roof fails, there's no money, and everyone gets a surprise bill. Chicago adds high property taxes and old buildings (porches, tuckpointing, basements that flood).

## A co-op operating budget
**Income:** carrying charges from members, plus any rent or other income.
**Expenses:**
- Mortgage principal and interest
- Property taxes (net of homestead exemptions passed through to members)
- Master insurance (property, liability, directors and officers)
- Utilities the co-op pays (water, common areas, maybe heat)
- Maintenance and repairs. A common rule of thumb is about 1 to 2% of property value per year; older buildings are on the high side.
- **Replacement reserve contribution**, from a reserve study
- Admin: bookkeeping, tax preparation, legal, bank fees

See the [worked example](../worksheets/scenario-staggered-entry.md#monthly-carrying-charges) for illustrative numbers.

## Splitting carrying charges
Options: equally per household, by square footage, by number of bedrooms, or by share count. Equal is simplest. Square footage is fairer if units differ a lot. Write down the rule and how it changes if units are renovated.

## Reserves
- **Operating reserve:** 3 to 6 months of expenses, for vacancies, late payments, and surprises.
- **Replacement reserve:** for big-ticket items such as the roof, boiler, water heater, windows, porches, tuckpointing, and sewer line. Sized by a **reserve study**: a list of each component with its remaining life and replacement cost. A professional study costs roughly $3k–$10k. For a small building we can start with a DIY version and get a pro to review it.
- **Vacancy/buyout reserve:** specific to our joining-and-leaving model (Step 5).
