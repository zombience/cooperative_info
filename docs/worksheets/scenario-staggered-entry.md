# Worked example: four members now, two later

> **Illustrative numbers only.** These are made up to show the mechanics, not a recommendation or a quote. Replace them with real figures once you have a target property, lender terms, and tax estimates. Have your financial professional check the final version.

This walks through the situation our group actually has: six memberships, four households buying in now, two joining in about two years.

## Assumptions

| Item | Amount | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase price | $750,000 | e.g. a Chicago 2-flat/3-flat or large house |
| Closing costs (~3%) | $22,500 | lender, title, legal, transfer taxes, inspections |
| Initial repairs | $40,000 | from inspection report |
| Starting reserves | $30,000 | operating + replacement cushion |
| **Total project cost** | **$842,500** | |
| Blanket mortgage (75% of price) | $562,500 | 30-year, 6.75% fixed |
| **Equity needed at closing** | **$280,000** | total cost minus loan |
| Equity per membership if 6 equal shares | $46,667 | |

## The problem
If all six shares were sold today, each household would put in about $46,667. But only four households are buying now. Someone has to cover the other ~$93,333 until the later two join.

## Four ways to bridge the gap

### Option A: Founders front the money as member loans
- Each founder buys one share for **$46,667** and lends the co-op **$23,333**, for $70,000 each.
- The two unsold shares stay with the co-op as treasury shares.
- When the later members join, they buy their shares at the formula price, and the co-op uses that money to repay the member loans.
- If the share formula grows 3% a year and the member loans earn 3% a year, the numbers line up: in two years each share sells for about **$49,509**, so two shares bring in **$99,017**, which is exactly what the co-op owes on the $93,333 of loans with interest.

**Good:** clean, fair to everyone, founders are made whole. **Watch:** founders need more cash upfront; what if the later members don't join? (Bylaws should say the co-op can sell those shares to someone else, and the loans are repaid then.)

### Option B: Later members pay a deposit now
- The two future members sign subscription agreements and pay a deposit now (say $10,000 each), with the rest due when they move in.
- Founders cover the remaining ~$73,333 as in Option A.

**Good:** everyone has skin in the game from day one; less upfront strain on founders. **Watch:** what happens to the deposit if a future member backs out? Write it down.

### Option C: Founders buy the extra shares outright
- The four founders buy all six shares now (1.5 each, or in whatever proportion they agree), then sell the two extra shares to the later members at the formula price.

**Good:** the co-op keeps one class of stock with every share owned by a member, which matters for the Section 216 tax treatment. **Watch:** founders hold an asset they must resell, and every share transfer is subject to Chicago transfer tax.

### Option D: Borrow more, pay it down later
- The co-op takes a larger loan or a second short-term loan to cover the gap, and later members' buy-ins pay it down.

**Good:** least cash from founders. **Watch:** many lenders cap LTV around 75 to 80% for co-ops; a second loan raises monthly costs for the four founders in the meantime, and it's riskier if rates or values move.

## Monthly carrying charges

| Monthly cost | Amount |
|---|---|
| Mortgage payment (principal + interest) | $3,648 |
| Property taxes (~$13,500/yr) | $1,125 |
| Insurance | $450 |
| Maintenance and repairs (~1% of value/yr) | $625 |
| Replacement reserve contribution | $500 |
| Shared utilities (water, common electric) | $300 |
| Admin, bookkeeping, misc. | $150 |
| **Total** | **$6,798** |

How that splits depends on who lives in the two spaces reserved for the later members:

| Scenario | Per founding household / month |
|---|---|
| All six households paying equal shares | $1,133 |
| Four households, two spaces empty | $1,700 |
| Four households, two spaces rented to tenants at $1,100 each | $1,150 |

Renting the spaces to non-members (possibly the future members themselves, as tenants) keeps founders' costs close to the long-run number. **But be careful.** Under IRC § 216, a co-op generally needs 80% of its income to come from member-residents for members to deduct their share of mortgage interest and property taxes. Rent from two of six spaces could break that test. Rented units also likely fall under the Chicago Residential Landlord and Tenant Ordinance. Raise both with the lawyer and the tax professional before choosing this route.

## What happens when someone leaves

Using a limited-equity formula of "price paid + 3% per year + approved improvements":

- A founder who paid $46,667 and leaves after 6 years would get back about $46,667 × 1.03⁶ ≈ **$55,722**, plus credit for improvements the board approved.
- The co-op needs an incoming member (or cash) to pay that. Common bylaw rules: the co-op has 6 to 12 months to find a buyer; the departing member keeps paying carrying charges until then, or the co-op takes over the unit and pays out in installments.
- Under market-rate, the share would sell for whatever a buyer will pay. That could be much more, or less in a down market.
