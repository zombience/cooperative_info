# Worked example: two households now, one later

!!! warning "Illustrative numbers only"
    These figures are made up to show how the mechanics work. They are not a recommendation or a quote. Replace them with real numbers once you have a target property, loan terms, and tax estimates, and have your financial professional check the final version.

This walks through our actual situation. We are **three households** (three couples, six people). Two households are buying in now, and one plans to join in about two years. We're looking at a Chicago **3-flat** (one unit per household) or a **4-flat** (one extra unit). The main example uses a 3-flat. The 4-flat variant comes after it.

## Assumptions (3-flat)

| Item | Amount | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase price | $750,000 | a Chicago 3-flat |
| Closing costs (~3%) | $22,500 | lender, title, legal, transfer taxes, inspections |
| Initial repairs | $40,000 | from the inspection report |
| Starting reserves | $30,000 | operating cushion plus replacement savings |
| **Total project cost** | **$842,500** | |
| Mortgage on the whole building (75% of price) | $562,500 | 30-year, 6.75% fixed |
| **Cash needed at closing** | **$280,000** | total cost minus the loan |
| Cash per household if all 3 shares were sold now | $93,333 | about $46,667 per person |

## The problem
If all three shares were sold today, each household would put in about $93,333. But only two households are buying now. Someone has to cover the third share, about $93,333, until the later couple joins.

## Four ways to cover the gap

### Option A: The two founding households lend the money
- Each founding household buys one share for **$93,333** and lends the co-op **$46,667**, for $140,000 per household ($70,000 per person).
- The third share stays with the co-op as an unsold (treasury) share.
- When the later couple joins, they buy that share at the formula price, and the co-op uses the money to repay the loans.
- Suppose the share price formula grows 3% a year and the loans also earn 3% a year. In two years the share sells for about **$99,017**. The co-op owes the same amount on the $93,333 of loans plus interest, so the numbers line up exactly.

**Good:** clean and fair, and the founders are paid back in full. **Watch:** the founders need a lot more cash up front. What if the later couple doesn't join? The bylaws should let the co-op sell the share to someone else and repay the loans from that sale.

### Option B: The later couple pays a deposit now
- The later couple signs a purchase agreement (a subscription agreement) and pays a deposit now, say $20,000. The rest is due when they move in.
- The founders cover the remaining ~$73,333, as in Option A (about $36,667 extra per founding household).

**Good:** everyone has money in from day one, and less strain on the founders. **Watch:** what happens to the deposit if the later couple backs out? Write it down.

### Option C: The founders buy the third share outright
- The two founding households buy all three shares now (1.5 each, or another split they agree on). Later they sell the extra share to the later couple at the formula price.

**Good:** the co-op keeps one class of stock, all owned by members, which matters for members' tax deductions (Section 216). **Watch:** the founders hold a share they must resell, and Chicago taxes every share transfer.

### Option D: Borrow more and pay it down later
- The co-op takes a bigger loan, or a second short-term loan, to cover the gap. The later couple's buy-in pays it down.

**Good:** the least cash from the founders. **Watch:** many co-op lenders cap the loan at 75 to 80% of the price. A second loan raises monthly costs for the two founding households in the meantime, and it's riskier if rates or values move.

## Monthly carrying charges (3-flat)

| Monthly cost | Amount |
|---|---|
| Mortgage payment (principal + interest) | $3,648 |
| Property taxes (~$13,500/yr) | $1,125 |
| Insurance | $450 |
| Maintenance and repairs (~1% of value per year) | $625 |
| Contribution to the replacement savings (reserve) | $500 |
| Shared utilities (water, common electric) | $300 |
| Admin, bookkeeping, misc. | $150 |
| **Total** | **$6,798** |

How that splits depends on what happens with the third unit until the later couple moves in:

| Scenario | Per founding household / month | Per person |
|---|---|---|
| All three households paying equal shares | $2,266 | ~$1,133 |
| Two households, third unit empty | $3,399 | ~$1,700 |
| Two households, third unit rented to a tenant at $1,800 | $2,499 | ~$1,250 |

Renting the third unit until the later couple moves in keeps the founders' costs close to the long-run number. The tenant could even be the later couple, renting before they buy. **But be careful.** Members can only deduct their share of the co-op's mortgage interest and property taxes if the co-op qualifies under IRC § 216. One of its tests needs 80% of the co-op's income to come from member-residents, and rent from one unit out of three could break that test. A rented unit also likely falls under the Chicago Residential Landlord and Tenant Ordinance. Raise both with the lawyer and the tax professional before choosing this route.

## Variant: a 4-flat with one rented unit

A 4-flat gives each household a unit, plus one unit the co-op rents out long-term. The rent helps cover costs, but it makes the co-op a landlord.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Purchase price | $900,000 |
| Total project cost (closing $27,000, repairs $50,000, reserves $35,000) | $1,012,000 |
| Mortgage (75% of price) | $675,000 |
| **Cash needed at closing** | **$337,000**, about $112,333 per household |
| Monthly costs (mortgage $4,378, taxes $1,350, insurance $550, maintenance $750, reserve $600, utilities $350, admin $200) | **$8,178** |
| Rent from the fourth unit | −$1,800 |
| **Per household when all three have joined** | **$2,126** (~$1,063 per person) |
| Per founding household before the later couple joins (third unit empty) | $3,189 |
| Per founding household before the later couple joins (third unit also rented) | $2,289 |

The 4-flat costs each household about the same per month as the 3-flat and gives the co-op an income cushion. In exchange, it needs **more cash up front**, makes the co-op a landlord **permanently**, and puts more pressure on the Section 216 tests (see above). The tax rule has alternative tests based on floor space and on how money is spent, so ask the tax professional whether a permanently rented fourth unit still lets us qualify.

## What happens when a household leaves

Suppose a capped (limited-equity) formula: the price paid, plus 3% a year, plus credit for improvements the board approved.

- A founding household that paid $93,333 and leaves after 6 years gets back about $93,333 × 1.03⁶ ≈ **$111,445**, plus any improvement credit.
- With only three households, **each one is about a third of the co-op's income**. A household leaving with no buyer lined up is a big hit. Common bylaw rules: the co-op has 6 to 12 months to find a buyer, and the departing household keeps paying its monthly charges until then. Or the co-op takes over the unit and pays the household out in installments.
- With market-rate shares, the share sells for whatever a buyer will pay, which could be much more or much less.
